# ASTR/PHSC 12600 — November 20 Project Status Update

**Working title:** Can AI Improve Exoplanet Detection? Reproducing a Transit Search and Testing Its Physical Failure Modes

**AI-assisted draft for student review.** I should verify the cited results and describe my own role before submitting. No course submission has been made.

My project examines what generative AI can and cannot contribute to exoplanet detection. When a planet passes in front of a star, its observed light becomes slightly dimmer. Repeating dips can reveal orbital periods, but eclipsing binary stars and telescope imperfections can produce deceptively similar patterns. I therefore wanted to reproduce an established transit search and test whether a small physical improvement actually helps on real astronomical data.

I started by reproducing the Box Least Squares (BLS) method, which recovered the already-known Kepler-69b period within about 0.066%. I then compared a simple signal-to-noise rule with a four-feature random forest using NASA's historical DR25 vetting catalog. The random forest agreed with historical NASA labels more often, but it also missed more historically labeled candidates. Those labels are not independent proof of real planets. Follow-up work on eclipsing binaries and artificial instrumental steps revealed further difficulties: a proposed odd/even eclipse check did not work reliably on six real binaries, and an added preprocessing mask sometimes removed real transit observations. I did not treat these apparent improvements as discoveries.

I next examined a different failure mode using archived Kepler measurements. A repetition-aware warning rule protected two genuine observed stellar eclipses from being mislabeled as sudden instrumental steps. It also missed examples outside the chosen search-period range. These tests were useful because they exposed exactly where the method worked and where it failed, instead of simply reporting its best examples.

The most recent work uses **real TESS spacecraft measurements and NASA's original ExoMiner++ v2.4.0 outputs**. I compared 248 authentic observed transit-event windows from 56 distinct TESS stars. The original NASA model was not modified. Subtracting TESS's recorded spacecraft-pointing estimate from the measured moment-centroid coordinates reduced the median local before/after centroid displacement for **44 of 56 stars**, while the displacement increased for **12**. The median source-paired reduction was **0.001035 detector pixels**, with a whole-star bootstrap 95% interval of **0.000738–0.001891 pixels**. This is a measurement-stability result, not proof of more accurate exoplanet classification.

On **November 12**, I added a follow-up sensitivity test to ask whether better stability might also suppress scientifically useful motion during a transit. Among the 56 stars, **32** showed both lower before/after motion and lower in-event centroid displacement; **12** showed improved stability without a smaller in-event displacement. In all 56 leave-one-star-out recalculations, the source-level median stability improvement remained positive. Replacing each star's event median with its event mean also left the number of improved stars at **44/56**, although the estimated magnitude changed. The full authentic-source workflow passed **261 regression tests**. These results are exploratory because the 56 stars were already selected and inspected. A smaller in-event shift could represent either reduced instrument noise or loss of a genuine signal, and I cannot distinguish those causes from these data.

This project connects directly to course lectures on **measuring astronomical distances and light** (Lesson 2), **time and precision of measurement** (Lesson 3), and **Newtonian gravity and Kepler's laws** (Lessons 5–6). In the paper I will explain how orbital period, transit depth, and measurement uncertainty enter the detection problem, with calculations, graphs and original observational evidence.

Before the **December 11** final deadline, I plan to shorten and polish the research paper, select the most explanatory figures, check scientific citations and complete the required project-elements checklist. My most important conclusion so far is that AI is useful for implementing and auditing astronomical analyses, but a reproducible change in a measurement is not necessarily a demonstrable improvement in identifying planets.
