# ASTR/PHSC 12600 — November 20 Project Status Update

**Working title:** Can AI Improve Exoplanet Detection? Reproducing a Transit Search and Testing Its Physical Failure Modes

**AI-assisted draft for student review.** I should verify the cited results and describe my own role before submitting. No course submission has been made.

My project examines what generative AI can and cannot contribute to exoplanet detection. When a planet passes in front of a star, its observed light becomes slightly dimmer. Repeating dips can reveal orbital periods, but eclipsing binary stars and telescope imperfections can produce deceptively similar patterns. I therefore wanted to reproduce an established transit search and test whether a small physical improvement actually helps on real astronomical data.

I started by reproducing the Box Least Squares (BLS) method, which recovered the already-known Kepler-69b period within about 0.066%. I then compared a simple signal-to-noise rule with a four-feature random forest using NASA's historical DR25 vetting catalog. The random forest agreed with historical NASA labels more often, but it also missed more historically labeled candidates. Those labels are not independent proof of real planets. Follow-up work on eclipsing binaries and artificial instrumental steps revealed further difficulties: a proposed odd/even eclipse check did not work reliably on six real binaries, and an added preprocessing mask sometimes removed real transit observations. I did not treat these apparent improvements as discoveries.

I next examined a different failure mode using archived Kepler measurements. A repetition-aware warning rule protected two genuine observed stellar eclipses from being mislabeled as sudden instrumental steps. It also missed examples outside the chosen search-period range. These tests were useful because they exposed exactly where the method worked and where it failed, instead of simply reporting its best examples.

The most recent work uses **real TESS spacecraft measurements and NASA's original ExoMiner++ v2.4.0 outputs**. I compared 248 authentic observed transit-event windows from 56 distinct TESS stars. The original NASA model was not modified. Subtracting TESS's recorded spacecraft-pointing estimate from the measured moment-centroid coordinates reduced the median local before/after centroid displacement for **44 of 56 stars**, while the displacement increased for **12**. The median source-paired reduction was **0.001035 detector pixels**, with a whole-star bootstrap 95% interval of **0.000738–0.001891 pixels**. This is a measurement-stability result, not proof of more accurate exoplanet classification.

On **November 12**, I added a follow-up sensitivity test to ask whether better stability might also suppress scientifically useful motion during a transit. Among the 56 stars, **32** showed both lower before/after motion and lower in-event centroid displacement; **12** showed improved stability without a smaller in-event displacement. In all 56 leave-one-star-out recalculations, the source-level median stability improvement remained positive. Replacing each star's event median with its event mean also left the number of improved stars at **44/56**, although the estimated magnitude changed. The full authentic-source workflow passed **261 regression tests**. These results are exploratory because the 56 stars were already selected and inspected. A smaller in-event shift could represent either reduced instrument noise or loss of a genuine signal, and I cannot distinguish those causes from these data.

I also tested whether the subtraction preserved a consistent **direction of in-event centroid movement** across separate observed events. This used the original signed CCD-coordinate vectors rather than only their magnitudes, again strictly matching the 248 original TESS event windows. Only 40 of the 56 stars had at least three complete observed events. Among them, corrected directional consistency increased for **23/40** sources and decreased for **17/40**. The median paired change was only **+0.00118** on a 0-to-1 scale, with a whole-star bootstrap 95% interval of **-0.00911 to +0.05048** (exploratory sign-test **p=0.430**). The source-reproducibility workflow passed **270 tests**. This **negative/inconclusive finding** is important: reduced instrument drift does not automatically mean a more consistent or physically accurate eclipse-location signal. I also prepared a separate protocol to seek externally established nearby eclipsing binaries, rather than treating historical model labels as off-target source truth. The new protocol itself does not represent a completed independent validation.

A separate public ExoFOP follow-up check offered a more physical way to plan future validation. The downloadable TOI catalog contained **1,322 broad false-positive labels**, from which 868 uninspected targets retained both reported time-series follow-up and usable catalog ephemerides. After reviewing 30 public observing-note records, I identified two **post-hoc** cases in which ground observers named a neighboring eclipsing star. For **TOI-2041.01** and **TOI-4255.01**, a separate nearby-star catalog independently matched the reported neighbor TIC IDs and separations at **23.82** and **5.10 arcseconds**, respectively. Neither case has yet been tested with my TESS centroid correction or independently reexamined ground-based photometry, so this does **not** establish improved source localization; it demonstrates a possible evidence-based direction for future work.

I then tested **original TESS two-minute SPOC light curves and target-pixel WCS** for the externally reported nearby-eclipse example **TOI-4255.01**. TOI-2041.01 had no matching two-minute SPOC source in our archive search, so it was not included. TOI-4255.01 had **8 complete measured eclipse windows in sector 61 and 10 in sector 62**. After subtracting the original recorded pointing estimate, the median event-centroid direction moved closer to the *expected direction away from the reported eclipsing neighbor* in both sectors (from **55.95° to 14.39°** and **153.84° to 15.56°**). However, a source-SHA-pinned 20,000-resample paired event analysis produced **uncertainty intervals that included zero improvement in both sectors**. Worse, one quarter-period off-eclipse control in sector 61 aligned almost as closely (13.80°) even without a median flux dip. The **303-test verified result is therefore exploratory and inconclusive**, not evidence that NASA's classifier or physical localization has been improved. These are two sectors of *one* star, not two independent validation stars.

Most recently, I tested whether the encouraging TOI-4255.01 directional alignment generalized. From archived ExoFOP observer notes and real MAST SPOC product availability, I **froze five additional host stars and their first available original two-minute observing sectors before opening their TESS centroid measurements**. The original data produced **63 complete event windows across five distinct stars**. One star had only two complete observed events, below our pre-existing three-event eligibility rule. Of the four evaluable stars, **two showed better alignment** with the idealized direction away from a note-named neighboring star after the pointing correction and **two showed worse alignment**. The median source-level angular improvement was **−6.65°**, with a 20,000 whole-host bootstrap 95% interval of **−47.16° to +17.95°** and an exploratory exact sign-test **p=1.0**. All **317 software tests passed**, with source FITS identities, WCS and missing-event denominators preserved. This did **not** reproduce a reliable improvement; the named nearby stars also remain candidate source identities pending direct ground-based light-curve verification, so the directional comparisons cannot be called proven physical localization accuracy. This negative result substantially strengthens the paper's critical interpretation.

I then checked the original **ground-based follow-up measurements** to see whether the nearby-star labels in the five-source experiment were physically supported. Original ExoFOP observing notes showed three ambiguous cases, including saturated or blended stars, conflicting detections and instrumental systematics. I downloaded **23 primary observer uploads** and the complete AstroImageJ ground light curves for the two most clearly reported neighboring events. At the original observer-predicted eclipse times, **TOI-1223.01's neighboring T6 aperture** showed a **39.0-parts-per-thousand** dip in 162 real observations, and **TOI-2250.01's T11 aperture** showed a **259.7-parts-per-thousand** dip in 84. Both remained positive when I compared separate pre-event, post-event and outside-event-trend baselines, with **336 science tests passing**. However, some other field-star apertures had large measurement drift, both target apertures were reported saturated, and the T6/T11 source-to-TIC sky mapping still requires an independent imaging check. The results support actual observed ground-aperture dimmings but **not a new exoplanet or proven improvement in TESS source localization**.
