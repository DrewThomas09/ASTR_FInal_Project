# Version 16: rebuilding the Kepler research story

**Drew Thomas | ASTR/PHSC 12600 | November 15, 2026**

The current Word/PDF paper (available in this project conversation) is six pages and contains 2,720 words of main research text, excluding its abstract, figure captions, references and completed course checklist. It follows the original November 5 submitted Kepler proposal. This record documents the research process and why the written paper now gives more space to decisions and failures than to secondary model analyses.

## Reading existing methods before making changes

The student's question was whether generative AI could make it practical to reproduce astronomical research methods. The starting point was not a new planet detector. Kovacs, Zucker and Mazeh (2002) explained the established Box Least Squares search for short periodic dips. Batalha et al. (2010) and Thompson et al. (2018) described why a detected dip requires further checks, including odd/even eclipse depths, secondary eclipses, contamination and pixel centroids. McCauliff et al. (2015) showed why combining Kepler measurements in a random forest could aid candidate vetting. ExoMiner further illustrated how much more physical information professional systems use. The paper explains what each reading taught the project, rather than presenting a detached literature list.

## What was tried, and what failed

1. The earliest simulated BLS test searched a 3.25-day injected transit only over a 2.8-3.8-day window. That was useful for checking software but too favorable to establish real-world detection performance. The later experiment used genuine NASA Kepler flux and a broader search range.
2. Initial direct MAST file retrieval returned HTTP 403. The program switched to the official MAST product route, parsed original Kepler long-cadence FITS, respected quality flags, and used a fixed 301-cadence median baseline. The input is NASA-processed PDC-SAP flux, not recreated raw detector pixels.
3. Kepler-69 b Quarter 8 gave a fitted period of 13.7133429855 days, versus NASA's 13.7223682 days, a 0.06577% relative difference. Separate shallow simulated box transits injected into real Kepler stellar noise succeeded in only 1/3 checks at 500 ppm, compared with 3/3 at 1500 and 4000 ppm. These small trials are not a population-wide detection rate. [Original real-data run](https://github.com/DrewThomas09/ASTR_FInal_Project/actions/runs/42319839037).
4. A preselected external Quarter 8 group recovered the intended planet's period for just 3/6 analyzed known planet-host targets. Four of six usable stellar binaries yielded half-period aliases, and the simple binary warning flagged none of them. Earlier success on a simple illustrative binary did not generalize. [Original cohort](FROZEN_REAL_13STAR_VALIDATION_RESULTS.md).
5. Some apparent misses were actually signals from other known planets in the same system. After seeing this, the project tried a second BLS pass on residual flux. It found the known Kepler-20 b period, 3.6963 days, on the second pass after detecting Kepler-20 c. Kepler-18 b was still not recovered after three passes. These deliberately selected development cases are not independent improvement validation. [Sequential search run](https://github.com/DrewThomas09/ASTR_FInal_Project/actions/runs/42321423938).
6. A more demanding predeclared test fitted periods using Quarter 6 observations, then examined later Quarter 7 data without changing timing. Six of ten planned known planet-host systems and both stellar-binary controls passed. Kepler-10, Kepler-30 and Kepler-27 failed for different recorded reasons, while Kepler-17 lacked usable data. Passing binaries showed that reliable repeated dimming is not the same as a planetary source. [Original frozen run](https://github.com/DrewThomas09/ASTR_FInal_Project/actions/runs/43512648584).
7. The separate machine-learning experiment used NASA's precomputed DR25 features, not measurements extracted by the custom BLS search. The forest matched historical labels for 81.6% of 1,619 held-out signals, versus 77.3% for logistic regression and 52.3% for a simple SNR cutoff. It rejected more false-positive labels but also missed more historical candidates. An attempted more-permissive long-period cutoff increased recovered historical candidates from 42/58 to 57/58, but accepted false positives rose from 28/87 to 64/87. Balanced accuracy became worse. [Model predictions](https://github.com/DrewThomas09/ASTR_FInal_Project/actions/runs/42513638366) and [negative cutoff experiment](VERIFIED_DR25_PERIOD_AWARE_THRESHOLD_NEGATIVE_2026_11_08.md).

The separate TESS pointing-correction analysis remains a short follow-up: the local motion measure was reduced for 44/56 stars but a corresponding source-identification gain was not demonstrated. The current paper removed the full eight-model feature-ablation graphic to make room for this documented sequence of scientific reading, reconstruction, failures, revisions and conclusions. That more technical analysis remains in the repository.

## Current paper structure and quality checks

**Research motivation and readings -> initially misleading synthetic success -> physics -> real NASA data handling -> broader Kepler failures -> iterative BLS attempt -> frozen later-quarter test -> catalog classification and unsuccessful threshold adjustment -> limited TESS test -> course synthesis and conclusion.**

The PDF was rendered from the editable Word paper. It has six pages, three source-backed original-data figures with captions, a ten-item bibliography, and the completed professor-provided project checklist. The source-specific paper and run links above support the claims; no new exoplanets or end-to-end classifier performance are claimed.

The paper's main answer is that AI made recognized astronomical methods practical to implement and investigate, while interpreting a recurring dip, verifying that it returns in later measurements, and identifying its physical cause required separate evidence.
