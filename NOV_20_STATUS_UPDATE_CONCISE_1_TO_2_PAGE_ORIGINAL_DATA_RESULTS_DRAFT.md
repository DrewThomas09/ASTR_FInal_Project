# Project Status Update
**Drew Thomas | ASTR/PHSC 12600 | Professor Buzasi | Prepared November 17, 2026**
**Testing AI in Exoplanet Research: Rebuilding a Transit Search with Kepler Data**

## What I have accomplished
Since submitting my November 5 proposal, I have moved beyond asking whether generative AI can produce functioning astronomy code. I now have a program that works on actual NASA Kepler observations, and I have tested what happens when it encounters more difficult signals. The original plan is still the center of my project: reproduce an established transit-search technique, compare a small machine-learning screen with a simpler rule, and examine where each approach falls short. What changed was my understanding of how much verification is needed before a promising result can be treated as meaningful.

I began by reading the original Box Least Squares (BLS) paper by Kovács, Zucker and Mazeh, along with research on Kepler's false-positive checks by Batalha and Thompson. The first version of my code searched for a simulated 3.25-day transit within a narrow 2.8-to-3.8-day range. It found the signal, but that was not very convincing: the test already restricted the answer to roughly the correct period, and simulated noise was much easier than real stellar observations. I decided that a meaningful reconstruction needed wider period searches, genuine telescope data, and examples designed to challenge the program.

Moving to NASA data created a more practical obstacle. A direct Kepler file download failed, so I changed the code to use an official MAST product route. I also had to learn how to read FITS files, filter missing or quality-flagged measurements, and remove slow brightness variations without erasing short transits. The resulting program uses NASA's already-processed PDC-SAP flux and a fixed running-median correction before applying Astropy's established BLS algorithm. I am not claiming to have invented BLS or reconstructed the original detector pixels.

## Kepler results and the first failures
The first success was Kepler-69 b. From Quarter 8 measurements, the code returned a period of 13.713343 days, versus the published 13.722368 days, a difference of 0.0658%. However, that close match did not generalize automatically. In a preselected group, it recovered the intended planet's period for three of six known planet-host targets. Four of six usable eclipsing binaries produced periods close to half the stars' actual orbital periods, and the simple binary-warning check failed on all six. In two multi-planet systems, the strongest signal belonged to a different known planet. I tried fitting and subtracting that strong dip before searching again: the second pass recovered the previously known Kepler-20 b period, while the intended Kepler-18 b still was not recovered after three passes. These were useful development attempts, not evidence of improved survey-wide detection performance.

## Testing later observations and screening signals
I then ran a stricter experiment: fit the period and predicted event times from Kepler Quarter 6 only, and test them on later Quarter 7 observations without changing the fitted timing. Six of ten planned known planet-host systems met the original rule, three did not, and one lacked usable Quarter 7 data. Both of the two stellar-eclipse controls also passed. This was probably the most important finding for understanding the astronomy. The code could predict that a star would dim at the expected time, yet the event could still be caused by another star rather than a planet.

The separate machine-learning experiment used four measurements already calculated by NASA: period, duration, transit depth, and signal-to-noise ratio. On 1,619 signals held out by host star, the random forest matched NASA's historical candidate/false-positive decisions 81.6% of the time. Logistic regression matched 77.3%, and a simple SNR threshold matched 52.3%. The forest accepted substantially fewer historical false positives, but it also missed more historical candidate labels than the SNR rule. I tested a more permissive cutoff for longer periods; candidate recovery increased, but false positives increased even more, reducing balanced accuracy. These labels are earlier NASA screening decisions, not independent confirmation of real planets, and this classifier does not directly process signals from my BLS program.

I also tested one smaller idea prompted by reading about blended stars: subtracting recorded telescope pointing motion from centroid measurements in TESS data. A local motion measure decreased for 44 of 56 stars, but further checks did not establish more accurate identification of the dimming source. I have kept this as a supplementary result rather than turning it into the main project.
