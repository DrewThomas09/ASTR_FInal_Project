# November 20 Project Status Update
**Drew Thomas | ASTR 12600 | Professor Buzasi**

**Testing AI's Impact in Exoplanet Research**

My November 5 proposal asked whether generative AI could help reproduce or improve established methods used to identify exoplanets. I used original NASA Kepler observations to implement a conventional transit search, then tested a small machine-learning model against a simpler screening rule. I also tried one separate telescope-measurement adjustment. The main experiments remain the Kepler search and classification work.

**Reproducing a real planet search.** A planet passing in front of a star causes a short dip in the measured brightness. I used AI to help develop Python code for Box Least Squares (BLS), a standard technique for identifying repeating dips. Applied to real Kepler-69 b observations, the program recovered a period of **13.713 days** versus the published **13.722 days**, a difference of **0.066%**. A broader set of preselected Kepler targets was less successful. It recovered the intended period for only three of six known planet-host stars, and four of six analyzed eclipsing binaries produced periods close to half their true stellar orbital periods. That showed why a strong repeating pattern is not automatically a planet.

**Testing a period on later, untouched observations.** I then used a test specified before its final run. The program fitted a period and predicted dip times using Kepler **Quarter 6 only**, then held those values fixed and examined the actual Quarter 7 light curves. The success rule required sufficiently observed events, repeated local brightness dips, and a comparison with alternative phases of the same star. **Six of ten preselected known planet-host stars passed**, while three did not and one lacked usable later-quarter data. **Both of the two known stellar-binary controls also passed.** This was an important result: the code could predict repeating dimming in later telescope measurements, but prediction alone still could not identify whether a planet or another star caused it.

**Building a screening model.** I used NASA's Kepler catalog to train a four-feature random forest on historical candidate and false-positive labels. The training, validation, and testing sets had separate stars. On 1,619 held-out signals, the forest agreed with those earlier screening labels **81.6%** of the time, versus **52.3%** for the SNR-only rule and **77.3%** for a logistic model using the same four measurements. The forest accepted far fewer historical false positives, but it also rejected more historical candidates than the signal-strength rule. It was screening NASA's already extracted catalog measurements, not classifying raw detections from my own BLS program.

An additional examination explained part of the difference. Among 391 signals with very high reported SNR, **312 were historically labeled false positives**. The forest correctly rejected 291 of those, but also rejected 24 of 79 historically candidate-labeled signals. Large, deep stellar eclipses can be especially easy to detect without being planetary transits. I also tried different decision thresholds for long-period signals. Those thresholds recovered more historical candidates, but accepted enough additional false positives to reduce balanced accuracy. The adjustment was therefore not an overall improvement.

**A smaller measurement experiment.** After reading about false positives caused by nearby or blended stars, I tested whether subtracting recorded TESS telescope pointing movement made measured stellar centroids steadier. A local movement measure improved for **44 of 56 stars**. However, tests using a separate small group of possible nearby-source eclipses did not demonstrate reliable improvement in identifying the actual dimming star. A measurement can look less noisy without producing a better astronomical interpretation.

The research connects to **Lesson 2** through flux and telescope resolution, **Lesson 3** through reliable event timing, **Lessons 5-6** through Kepler's laws and orbital periods, and **Lab 1/Appendix B** through measurement uncertainty. Kovács, Zucker and Mazeh (2002) explained the BLS method; McCauliff and colleagues (2015) helped motivate the forest; and the Kepler DR25 vetting work of Thompson and colleagues (2018) explained why candidate labels are not independently confirmed planets.

**Current conclusion.** AI helped make real astronomical methods accessible enough to implement and test, but the strongest insight came from identifying where the methods fail. The Kepler program recovered real periodic signals and predicted some of them in later observations, while the model screened historical catalog decisions better than a simple rule. Neither a highly regular dip nor a highly confident model score independently establishes a planet. The final report now incorporates the original light curves, complete success and failure records, uncertainty, literature, and course connections.
