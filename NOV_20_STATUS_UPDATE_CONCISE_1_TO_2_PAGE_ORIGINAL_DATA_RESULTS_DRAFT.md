# November 20 Project Status Update

**ASTR/PHSC 12600 — Working title: Can AI Improve Exoplanet Detection? An Original-Data Test of Transit Vetting and Telescope Pointing**

My final project asks whether generative AI can help reproduce an existing exoplanet-detection method and meaningfully improve its physical reliability. An exoplanet can cause a small, periodic drop in a star's observed light. But an eclipsing neighboring star can produce a similar signal, and changing telescope pointing can alter the measured position of the light. I am studying how to distinguish an improvement in a calculated measurement from a genuine improvement in identifying planets.

I began by implementing the Box Least Squares transit search and recovered the known Kepler-69b orbital period to within about 0.066%. I also compared simple statistical screening with a random forest on NASA's historical Kepler DR25 records, then examined known eclipsing binaries and instrumental artifacts. These experiments showed that a machine-learning or preprocessing change can improve one metric while harming a different, scientifically important measure.

For the main original-data investigation, I used authentic NASA/TESS two-minute FITS files and the published ExoMiner++ work as a reference. TESS records a star's brightness, its flux-weighted detector position, and estimates of the spacecraft's pointing motion. I tested a simple adjustment that subtracts the recorded pointing motion from the measured centroid position, without changing NASA's pretrained neural network. In an original 56-star sample, this reduced a local before/after centroid drift measure for 44 stars. But the improvement was not consistently reflected in the direction of the centroid shift during transit, which is needed to identify whether a signal came from a neighboring star.

To test that distinction, I froze five new false-positive follow-up candidates **before** examining their TESS centroid values. Across 63 genuine event windows, only four stars met the minimum event-count requirement; two improved directionally after correction and two worsened. Original ground-based observation notes and actual follow-up files also revealed that several nearby-star identities were ambiguous. I therefore recovered the underlying telescope photometry for two stronger cases. The original ground measurements showed eclipses of about **3.90%** in a neighboring aperture for TOI-1223.01 and **25.97%** for TOI-2250.01. The dips persisted under several baseline choices. Using actual plate-solved ground images, I matched these apertures to independently cataloged nearby stars within 0.44 and 2.20 arcseconds.

I also formed a separate five-star control sample by matching confirmed transiting planets in NASA's Exoplanet Archive to known-planet records in ExoFOP. I fixed each star and its TESS observing sector before measuring any centroid outcomes. All five systems had authentic original TESS observations, comprising **46 complete transits**. The correction made the in-transit centroid vector smaller for all five hosts, but improved the local drift measure for only four. In one confirmed planet, CoRoT-1 b, the local drift became considerably worse. Making a centroid shift smaller is not necessarily better: a genuine planet transit can itself cause a physical shift if its host is blended with another star.

Finally, I tested whether the two ground-measured nearby eclipses could physically account for the much smaller TESS brightness dips. A simple two-star flux-dilution calculation predicted about **293 versus 351 parts per million** observed for TOI-1223.01, and **92.5 versus 98.7 parts per million** observed for TOI-2250.01. The second agreement is encouraging but retrospective and depends on simplifying assumptions about how much light enters the detector aperture.

I also tested source localization directly. Both neighboring stars fell within the original TESS photometry and centroid pixel masks, but my simple pixelwise eclipse difference images favored the bright primary, not the reported neighbor. NASA's own, more sophisticated **Data Validation** reports provide a stronger check. I retrieved **12 original official XML reports** (six overlapping search runs for each of the two stars), verified their detected transit periods and phases matched the original TOIs, and examined the actual first-sector point-response-function source estimates. Only **six** of the 12 repeated-run fits were valid; **all six nominally fell closer to the primary than the reported eclipsing neighbor**. Results also changed markedly across reprocessing campaigns. That does not disprove the separately measured ground eclipse, but neither the simple nor official TESS image analysis independently identifies the neighbor as its source.

My conclusion is cautious: AI helped build and verify original-data experiments, but **I have not shown improved planet/false-positive classification or discovered an exoplanet**. The final paper will compare why these different measures disagree and distinguish genuine physical evidence from changes in a calculation.
