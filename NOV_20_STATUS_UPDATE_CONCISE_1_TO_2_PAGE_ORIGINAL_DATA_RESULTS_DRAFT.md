# November 20 Project Status Update
**Drew Thomas · ASTR 12600 · Professor Buzasi**

**Project: Testing AI's Impact in Exoplanet Research**

My original proposal asked whether AI could help reproduce or improve an established way of finding exoplanets. I wanted to use NASA's Kepler data to build a working planet-search program, compare machine learning with a simpler method, and see where AI was useful and where human scientific judgment was still necessary. That is still the main question of my project.

**First, I used AI to help reproduce an established planet-search technique.** When a planet passes in front of a star, the star's light gets slightly dimmer. If the dip repeats regularly, it might mean that a planet is orbiting the star. I used generative AI to help write Python code for Box Least Squares (BLS), a standard method for finding repeating dips in a star's brightness. To check it, I tested the program on real Kepler observations of a known planet, Kepler-69 b. The code estimated an orbital period of **13.713 days**, compared with the published **13.722 days**, a difference of only **0.066%**. This showed that AI helped me build a functioning version of a real astronomy technique. It was not a new planet discovery.

**Second, I tested whether machine learning could do a better job of separating promising signals from false alarms.** Finding a dip is not enough because eclipsing stars and telescope problems can resemble planetary transits. I used **8,054** records from NASA's Kepler catalog, divided into separate training, validation, and testing groups. I compared a simple rule based on signal strength against a random-forest model that considered four measurements: orbital period, dip duration, dip depth, and signal strength. On **1,619 held-out signals**, the simple method agreed with NASA's older classifications **52.3%** of the time, while the machine-learning model agreed **81.6%** of the time. The machine-learning model also incorrectly accepted far fewer signals NASA had labeled false positives (**189 versus 695**). However, it rejected more signals NASA had labeled candidates (**109 versus 77**). These are comparisons with NASA's historical screening labels, not proof that the model can confirm real planets.

**Third, I looked for one small change worth testing after studying the limits of existing methods.** Rather than claiming to invent a better version of NASA's full detection system, I tested a simpler question: what happens if we subtract the telescope's recorded movement from the measured movement of the star's center of light? For this additional experiment I used NASA's **TESS** telescope, which publishes the measurements needed for that correction. This is a separate follow-up, not a change to the Kepler machine-learning model.

The adjusted measurement was steadier for **44 of 56 TESS stars** according to our local movement measure. At first that seemed promising. But more detailed checks showed that a smaller movement is not automatically better. If two nearby stars blend together and one dims, the apparent center of their light can move for a real astronomical reason. An adjustment could remove useful information along with telescope noise. In a separate set of four usable suspected nearby-eclipse systems, the adjustment improved the direction of the shift in only **two** and made it worse in the other **two**. We therefore **could not show that this adjustment reliably improved planet identification**.

**My conclusion so far:** AI made real astronomy programming more accessible. It helped me reproduce a standard search method and train a small classification model that was much better than a simple signal-strength rule at matching historical Kepler labels. The attempted telescope adjustment gave mixed results and showed why a cleaner measurement is not necessarily a more accurate scientific conclusion. The final report will explain these three experiments, include actual NASA-based graphs, compare the methods fairly, and discuss what still required scientific interpretation.
