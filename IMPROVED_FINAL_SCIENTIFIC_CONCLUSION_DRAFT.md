## Proposed conclusion for the final report (accurate with presently verified evidence)

The aim of this project was not simply to reproduce a transit-detection algorithm, but to examine whether an AI-assisted research process could identify a genuine weakness in astronomical signal analysis and develop a better way to address it. I began with a standard Box Least Squares search and a small machine-learning classifier trained to distinguish historical NASA Kepler candidate and false-positive labels. Those experiments reproduced known orbital signals, and the four-feature random forest agreed significantly better with NASA's historical labels than either an SNR-only rule or a four-feature logistic regression. Its balanced accuracy on the grouped-by-star holdout was 81.29%, compared with 50.23% and 76.61%, respectively. However, this was agreement with an earlier vetting catalog, not independent proof that more real exoplanets had been detected.

The more important finding emerged when I examined the original telescope measurements rather than the catalog alone. A local instrument-discontinuity detector sometimes flagged real stellar eclipses and would have removed measurements during those events. I investigated whether a repeating brightness pattern could protect astrophysical observations from such removal. On a newly selected set of 13 Kepler stars, the fixed safeguard protected two warnings in two eclipsing-binary systems, preserving 42 measured cadences. The recovered repetition periods were close to the independently published binary orbital periods or their harmonics, and original NASA light curves showed actual stellar eclipses near both protected events. This demonstrated a meaningful data-analysis failure mode: instrumental preprocessing can confuse valid astrophysical variability with detector defects.

These findings must be separated from evidence of improved planetary discovery. The preserved events were **stellar eclipses**, not newly detected planetary transits. The method missed other real eclipses, including repeated events on Kepler-34 beyond its predeclared 22-day period range, and the independent cohort contained no labeled genuine sudden pixel sensitivity dropouts for measuring instrument-defect safety. A separate attempt to recover more long-period historical candidate labels with period-specific classifier thresholds increased false-positive classifications enough to reduce balanced accuracy. Neither positive nor negative results justify claiming that this project outperforms NASA's detection pipeline.

The strongest contribution is therefore a **reproducible, physically grounded investigation of where transit-search systems can fail**, together with an explicit next-step hypothesis: ambiguous photometric events should be tested for local recovery, recurrence, future predictive coherence and instrumental evidence before data are permanently discarded. A NASA-useful follow-up would compare a reversible, physics-aware artifact treatment against BLS, Transit Least Squares and relevant NASA pipeline controls on independent real photometry with injected planetary ground truth, reporting recovered planets **at a fixed false-alarm rate**, confidence intervals and per-event failures. AI made rapid implementation, experiment design and auditing possible, but the failed external validations show why physics, source provenance and independent tests—not model complexity or a small p-value alone—must determine whether an apparent discovery improvement is scientifically reliable.
